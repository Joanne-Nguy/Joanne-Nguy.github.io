#### Abstract

A recipe for a simple no-knead sourdough focaccia with toppings reminiscent of pizza.

#### Materials

- Straight-walled jar
- Weighing scale
- Spatula
- Rubber band
- Medium-sized mixing bowl
- Whisk or fork
- Plastic wrap or Shower cap
- 9x13" Baking pan
- Wire rack

#### Procedure

##### Prepare Active Sourdough Starter
**Hands-on**: 5 mins | **Incubation**: 8-12h (For a 1:2:2 feeding)
1. In a straight-walled jar, add 25g of refrigerated or fresh sourdough starter and 50g of water.
2. Close the lid and shake jar contents until thoroughly mixed.
3. Add 50g of flour and mix with a spatula until there are no more streaks of flour.
4. Place a rubber band at the level of the sourdough starter. Let sourdough starter rest until at least doubled in height from the rubber band.
- Starter doubling time will be dependent on ambient temperature, temperature of ingredients added and starter activeness. 
- Feeding the sourdough starter at a 1:2:2 ratio will take approximately 8-12h for starter from the fridge to double in size.
- Depending on the incubation time that works best for your schedule, the sourdough can be fed at a 1:1:1 ratio for 4-6h until doubled or 1:5:5 for 12-18h until doubled.

##### Prepare Dough for Cold Fermentation
**Hands-on**: 10 mins | **Incubation**: 30 mins and 12h
1. From the active starter prepared above, add 100g to a medium mixing bowl.
- Starter may be fed according to the section above, [Prepare Active Sourdough Starter](http://joanne-nguy.github.io/blog/2025/sundried-focaccia/#prepare-active-sourdough-starter), then stored in the fridge for about 1 week.
2. Add filtered water and salt.
3. Whisk until there are no chunks of starter at the bottom of the bowl.
4. Pour in the flour and mix with the spatula to ensure no dry streaks of flour in the dough.
5. Let the dough rest for 1 hour.
- This step is fermentolysis with salt, which allows the flour to hydrate and makes it easier to work with.
1. Perform one set of 8-10 stretch and folds, pulling each "corner" of the dough before the tearing point and stretching it across the top of the dough.
2. Shape the dough into a dome shape, scraping down the sides of the bowl.
3. Using your hands, pour 2 Tbsp of olive oil onto the top of the dough and coat evenly.
- This layer of olive oil will prevent the dough from drying out during the cold fermentation step.
1. Cover the mixing bowl with a plastic wrap or shower cap.
2. Place the covered bowl in the fridge for an overnight cold fermentation.
- The dough can be fermented for as little as 8h or as much as 48h to develop more of the sourdough tang. Cold fermentation past 72h will cause the dough to loose elasticity.

##### Prepare Dough for Bulk Fermentation and Baking
**Hands-on**: 10 mins | **Incubation**: 4-6h and 45-50 mins
1. Coat the baking pan evenly with olive oil.
2. Remove the covered bowl with your dough from the refrigerator and gently pour contents into the oiled baking pan. The dough should pour cleanly, if not, scrape the sides of the bowl.
3. Gently stretch the corners of the dough to fill in the bottom of the pan.
4. Place in a warm area and allow to bulk ferment for 4-6h.
- The duration of bulk fermentation depends on the ambient temperature. A warm environment can be created with a boiling pot of water contained in the oven on a separate rack from the proofing dough.
- The dough is ready after bulk fermentation if the dough rises ~50% or when a thumbprint is poked into the dough and it springs back slowly.
5. Pre-heat the oven to 425<sup>o</sup>F
6. Evenly add sun-dried tomatoes with the olive oil marinade, oregano, chili flakes and garlic powder. 
7. Using all ten fingers, dimple the dough all the way down to the pan. 
- This will help air bubbles migrate to the top of the bread without popping them.
8. Bake at 425<sup>o</sup>F for 35-40 mins or until the crust is browned.
9. Remove pan from heat and let rest for 10 mins in the pan.
10. Transfer focaccia to a wire rack to cool.
11. Serve warm or double baked!
- Optionally, top with flaky sea salt to taste.

After recently coming into the possession of a sourdough starter, I've been making the basic rosemary focaccia recipe and wanted to change it up with a pizza-inspired focaccia with acidic sun-dried tomatoes as the star ingredient, with support from dried oregano, garlic powder and chili flakes. I was instantly transported to my favorite corner pizza shop. With a flour hydration of 78%, this recipe produces a focaccia that has a lovely airy and open crumb that seems to melt in your mouth following the satisfying crunch of an olive oil-toasted crust.

Focaccia is a forgiving recipe that will continue to give you consistent results even with inconsistent preparations. Since starting this journey, I have been experimenting with getting to know how my starter behaves in my specific kitchen conditions. Not everyone will have the same ambient environment and that can wildly affect how a recipe will behave in your kitchen. This focaccia was made in a kitchen that is around 70-75<sup>o</sup>F with humidity consistently 60%+. Since my kitchen is on the cooler side, I have been placing my starter and dough in the warm cabinet under my sink for the fermentolysis step and on trivets rather than the cold counters for the bulk fermentation. Even humidity in the air will affect how the dough will hydrate--although I have been using the standard hydration percentage just fine, I will cold ferment the dough covered by plastic wrap, which will not allow moisture to be wicked from the top of the dough. As for the dough itself, I found that differences of ±1% of the ingredients resulted in nearly identical loaves (scale-less bakers rejoice!).

(Update 10/23/25) As my kitchen cools further with the fall chill, I have been experimenting with more ways to proof dough in my environment. My starter now sits on top of the water boiler under my sink when it is not chilling in the fridge. The room temperature bulk ferments have been gradually increasing in time, which makes it difficult to work around my schedule. We want our sourdough to work for us, rather than the other way around, so my bulk fermentation now takes place in a closed oven with a pot of boiling water at the bottom to keep the ambient temperature comfortable for yeast activity. I may also try experimenting with starting the bulk fermentation the night before (while covered, since I have a cat that loves raw sourdough for whatever reason).

This update is also the 2-month mark of weekly sourdough focaccia in my kitchen. I've learned a lot about how sourdough works and how to better understand it. A really in-depth resource for sourdough in general can be found at [The Sourdough Framework](https://www.the-sourdough-framework.com/), where the author dives into the ins and outs of sourdough fermentation, as well as a detailed troubleshooting guide with helpful visuals. Once the foundation of knowledge was laid, it became easier to apply and know how to apply that knowledge in baking sourdough focaccia. My latest loaf is miles ahead of my first loaf, because with each loaf, I am making small adjustments - for example, a longer bulk fermentation because my last loaf was not as proofed as it could be, resulting in a springier loaf for this batch. Slowly but surely, I am understanding my starter, understanding my dough, and understanding my add-ins; documenting each step to ensure that details are not missed and so I can share this journey with you.

##### Topping Substitutions

For this recipe, I wanted to recreate how eating a pizza feels but without the mess of the sauce--plus I had sun-dried tomatoes sitting in my pantry for who knows how long, that were itching to be used. I've procured some fresh mozzarella and will be using that in my next batch of focaccia for a creamier, stretchy cheesiness that this recipe was frankly missing. The toppings as is are sufficient, but the addition of mozzarella should elevate it from pizza-inspired to to pizza-competitor.

The toppings chosen for a focaccia recipe can vary greatly, starting from anything readily-available in your herb garden or pantry, to more gourmet grocery store offerings. Since focaccia is a light tasting bread with tanginess from the sourdough and earthy, fruitiness of the olive oil, toppings that offer a bit of saltiness or umami wil greatly pair with it.

(Update 10/23/25) After experimenting with certain add-ins, I have discovered that it is much easier to add dried or dehydrated ones since they have minimal impact on the dough and bake time, even when folded in. Addition of ingredients that contain moisture, like vegetables, will increase bake time with the trade-off that the toppings may burn with increased bake time, so, those ingredients could be added near the end of baking instead to minimize added moisture content.
