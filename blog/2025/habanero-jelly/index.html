yuzu habanero pepper jelly

Created on April 02, 2025

2025   ·   cooking,   sweet   ·   recipes

Abstract

A recipe for a jelly preserve that has a kick from the habanero pepper and a tang from the yuzu.

Heavily inspired by Katie’s recipe.

Materials

  • Recommended: food-safe powder-free gloves
  • Saucepan
  • Small bowl
  • Sterilized spoon
  • Sterilized canning jar (>14 oz)

Reagents

  Ingredient Amount Preparation
Habanero 1/4 Cup Washed
Bell pepper 1 Cup Finely chopped and deseeded
Distilled vinegar 1/2 Cup None
Yuzu extract 2 Tbsp None
Pectin (Optional: sugar-free) 1 oz None
Granulated Sugar 2 Cups or follow instructions on the pectin box None


Procedure

Prepare Habaneros

Hands-on: 5 mins

  1. Don gloves, wash gloves with soap.
  2. Using a knife and cutting board, slice habaneros in half. Remove seeds and stem.
  3. Finely chop habaneros. The smaller the strips, the quicker the habanero will cook.
  4. Set aside in a small bowl. Discard gloves.
Prepare Jelly

Hands-on: 15 mins

  1. In a saucepan on high heat, add habanero and bell peppers.
  2. In a small bowl, mix yuzu extract and pectin together. Mixture will be thick like cornstarch and water.
  3. Add the pectin mixture to the saucepan and stir continuously. Bring to a boil and remove from heat.
  4. Add sugar and place on high heat. Return to rolling boil for 1 min.
  5. Remove from heat and skim off any foam
  6. Add to sterilized jar.
Preserve Jelly

Hands-on: 10 mins | Incubation: 2+ days

  1. Add jar to a pot and completely submerge with hot water. Boil for 5-10 mins depending on altitude.
  2. Remove from heat.
  3. Test seal by pressing the center of the lid. If lid springs back, repeat boiling process.
  4. Allow jar to cool to room temperature, about 12 h.
    • Jelly will continue to thicken for 2 days at room temperature.
    • Jelly may be stored at room temperature or in the fridge where it will thicken even more.
  5. Store jar upside down so that peppers are floating to the bottom of the jar.
  6. After 2 days, store jar right side up. Peppers in the jelly will be distributed evenly instead of floating to the top.
Serve Jelly
  1. Unseal the jar. You should hear a pop which indicates that the seal is still effective.
  2. Serve on toast or with crackers and cheese.
Yield 1 14 oz jar.
Storage Preserved jelly is shelf-stable for 3 years. Refrigerate after opening for up to 1 month.


Discussion

Jars of pepper jelly. Photo from Wikimedia.

Yuzu habanero pepper jelly was surprisingly a hit, as there was heat from the habanero, which was tempered by the sourness of the yuzu and sweetness of the jelly. A perfect addition to wine and cheese night. The jelly set soft and slightly runny from room temperature, but was the perfect thickness after a night of setting in the fridge. If you prefer a thicker jelly, try using sugar-free pectin or more sugar.

Variations

To my orange habaneros, I added yellow and orange bell peppers to keep with the same color scheme for a palette that was harmonious to the eyes. If that’s not a consideration for you, feel free to add any combination of hot peppers and bell peppers to your jelly.

To temper the spiciness, fruits may be added as well. Some fruits that go well with peppers are mango, strawberry and blueberry. The fruit will add a hint of sweetness to balance out the spicy heat of the peppers.

The acid in the jelly can be substituted as well. I used a combination of distilled vinegar and yuzu since I did not have apple cider vinegar on hand. The yuzu mimiced the depth of flavor I would be missing from the apple cider vinegar and added a slightly herbal, citrus note to the jelly without being too overpowering.

Spice Level

I chose habaneros, a pepper with a rating of 100-350k Scoville Heat Units (SCU) which is on the spicier side, since it was readily available to me for free at a farmer’s market. The resulting heat in the jelly was surprisingly bearable (coming from someone with a mild spice tolerance). Cooking down the peppers will reduce the heat, so the time cooked may be adjusted based on desired spice level. Yes, my tongue and mouth still burned, but it was nothing like the heat I felt after slicing the habaneros, washing my hands (3 times!) and touching my face - please be careful when handling peppers!

For comparison, here are the SCUs of some commonly eaten peppers:

Pepper Spiciness (SCU)
Bell pepper 0
Jalapeno pepper 2.5-10k
Cayenne pepper 25-50K
Thai chilies 50-100k
Habanero pepper 100k-350K
