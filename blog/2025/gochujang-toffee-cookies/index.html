gochujang toffee cookies

Created on September 12, 2025

2025   ·   cookies,   baking,   sweet,   ferments   ·   recipes

Abstract

An experimental take on New York Time’s popular gochujang caramel cookies using crunchy toffee bits and sourdough discard for a thicker cookie.

This recipe is a work in progress where I’ll document my findings here.

Materials

  • (Recommended) Weighing scale
  • Silicone spatula
  • Light-colored pan
  • 2 Mixing bowls (preferably heat-proof)
  • Large sifter
  • Measuring cups/spoons
  • Cookie scoop (2 Tbsp or other)
  • Freezer bag
  • 2 Large baking sheets
  • Cooling rack
  • Silicone cookie sheet
  • (Optional) Mixer with paddle attachment

Reagents

  Ingredient Amount Preparation
Unsalted Butter 1 cup (16 Tbsp; 226g) Room temperature
Packed light or dark brown sugar 1 and 1/4 cup (275g) None
Recommended: (Malted) milk powder 3 Tbsp None
Large eggs 2 Room temperature
Pure vanilla extract 2 Tsp None
All-purpose flour 2 and 1/3 cups (288g) Spooned & leveled
Baking soda 1 Tsp None
Kosher salt 1 Tsp None
Sourdough discard 1/4 cups (50g) Room temperature; recently discarded/fed sourdough preferred
Recommended: Heath Bar Bits O’ Brickle English Toffee 3/4 cup (100g) None
Gochujang 3 Tbsp (90g) None


Procedure

Prepare Wet Ingredients

Hands on: 15 mins | Incubation: 15-20 mins

  1. In a heat-proof bowl, add brown sugar and granulated sugar.
  2. Optional: Place the heat-proof bowl in an ice bath.
  3. Brown the butter:
    1. Heat a light-colored pan to medium heat on the stove.
    2. Cut softened butter into small, equally-sized slices.
    3. Once pan has heated up, add butter.
    4. Continuously stir with silicone spatula until cubes have melted.
    5. Listen for when the crackling subsides and foam starts to clear - milk solids (brown bits) will start forming on the bottom of the pan.
      • Until experienced, the baker can turn the heat down to medium-low for browning butter. The process will take longer, but it is less likely to burn the milk solids
    6. Add milk powder and toast for 20s until golden. Continuously stir with silicone spatula.
    7. Turn off the heat.
  4. Immediately take off heat and pour browned butter including milk solids into the heat-proof bowl with sugar.
  5. Chill the bowl in ice bath or fridge for 15-20mins until solid but still soft to touch.
Prepare Dry Ingredients

Hands on: 2 mins

  1. While waiting, sift flour, baking soda and salt into a mixing bowl.
  2. Briefly mix ingredients together. Set aside.
Combine Ingredients

Hands on: 5 mins

  1. Whisk brown butter and sugar on medium until combined ~1 min.
  2. Add vanilla and eggs.
  3. Whisk wet ingredients on high until fluffy ~2 mins.
  4. Whisk on low. Add sourdough starter and mix until incorporated, about 1 min.
  5. Add half of dry ingredients until combined. Add the rest of the dry ingredients until combined. Scrape down the sides of the bowl.
  6. Add toffee bits, if using. Mix with spatula until combined, ensuring bottom is well-mixed.
  7. Add 3 scoops of gochujang. Using a spatula, create thick ripples of gochujang in the dough with swirling motions, rather than mixing to incorporate it.
Chill Dough

Hands-on: 10 mins | Incubation: 4-48 h

  1. Cover mixing bowl and place in the fridge for at least 2 hours.
  2. Scoop cookie dough and place into a freezer bag. Let the dough rest for 24-48 hours.
Bake

Incubation: 22-29 mins

  1. Pre-heat the oven and bake at 350oF for 12-14 mins until bottom edges are golden brown.
    • Space cookies with 2” between each to allow room for spreading.
    • Pre-flatten the cookies for a dense cookie or bake cookie balls for a taller, fluffier cookie
    • Time will depend on factors such as cookie scoop size and texture preference. Try 13 mins first, then adjust from there.
  2. Allow cookies to cool on the baking sheet for 10-15 mins before moving to wire rack. Add flaky sea salt.
  3. Serve warm!
Yield 20-24 using a 2-Tbsp cookie scoop
Storage Cookies stay fresh for 3 days on the counter or 1 week in the fridge. Microwave for ~7s before enjoying.
Extra dough may be stored in the freezer for up to 6 months. When baking from frozen, pre-heat the oven to 350oF and add a minute to the bake time.


Discussion

Gochujang-caramel swirl cookies. Photo from New York Times.

I remember the first time I baked NYT’s gochujang caramel cookie–the cookie was unbelievably chewy, slightly cinnamon-y, which complemented the spicy umami of the gochujang. In this recipe, I leaned in on the caramel part, adding my signature brown butter + malted milk combination and crunchy toffee bits for that deep, toasty flavor. I found that adding brown sugar to the gochujang for the ripples created a runny mess, so I forwent the brown sugar this time and the gochujang seemed to hold up well without mixing into the dough as much this time around, creating those deep red ripples. Of course, this will make the cookie seem spicier without the additional crutch of brown sugar to mask it.

The superstar substitution is sourdough starter to add a bit of tang and airy texture, contrasting my usual cookie recipes.

Dough Composition and Texture

My classic cookie dough uses more flour than sugar by weight (313 flour vs 250 sugar) for a thicker cookie. In comparison, NYT’s recipe uses less flour than sugar (185 flour vs 200 sugar) for a flatter and crispier cookie. Let’s cut my cookie recipe in half so that the recipes are more comparable based on the same amount of butter and eggs:

Joanne's Recipe NYT's Recipe
Flour Weight (g) % Fat (Butter) % Liquid (Sugar) Flour Weight (g) % Fat (Butter) % Liquid (Sugar)
156.5 116 80 185 122 108

Using baker’s math, I calculated %fat and %liquid of the dough. In this example, I calculate %fat for my recipe:

\[\% fat = \frac{weight_{fat} }{weight_{flour}} \times 100\%\\ \% fat = \frac{226g \times 80\%} {156.5g} \times 100\%\\ \% fat = 116%\]

Overall, my cookie recipe uses less fat and less liquid content (sugar), relying on the structure of the flour to hold the cookie together in a thicker cookie.

Interestingly, NYT’s recipe called for a minimal chill time of 15-20mins, which in my experience–and very red kitchen counters, was quite the task to handle the wet, sticky dough. My preference is to chill the dough for at least 2h, but even with my changes in the dough composition, it was not long enough to counteract the liquid content of the gochujang and sourdough starter. I had to stop after scooping half my dough and continued after another 30 min chill. Of course, the overnight chill cannot be ommitted for the best flavor development–and I’m excited to observe the changes the sourdough starter will create!

Sourdough Background

I previously experimented with adding Tangzhong to my classic brown butter chocolate chip recipe, which added a heavenly chewiness to the cookie, which I’ve been loathe to reproduce due to the time and effort payoff. Recently, I’ve acquired a sourdough starter and have been itching to experiment adding it to my cookie recipes and what better way to enter the foray than with a long-awaited revisit with NYT’s gochujang caramel cookie recipe.

Similar to the Tangzhong, sourdough starter is partly liquid, which cookie recipes usually do not call for. In King Arthur’s recipe, 24g of flour is added to 113g of milk which is then cooked on the stove until it is a thick paste. This process gelatinizes the starches, which will allow the flour to hold more moisture, as well as to prevent the starches from recrystallizing, a process which causes bread to become stale and hard.

For a similar effect, I wanted to experiment with using sourdough starter which, in short, is a mixture of water and flour that contains a stable yeast and bacterial colony that feed on starches in the flour to produce carbon dioxide (lots of bubbles), lactic acid (think, dairy-smell) and acetic acid (vinegary-smell). As a result, when added to cookie dough, the sourdough starter may provide some benefits in terms of hydrating and fermenting the cookie dough in addition to providing the signature sourdough tang.

How Sourdough Affected This Recipe

I regularly keep my sourdough starter at 4C since I bake bread weekly and don’t need it to be active on the daily. For this particular recipe, I fed my starter 3 days prior and kept it in the fridge since. The starter doubled and the sides were bubbly before I stirred it and removed some for this recipe. The starter was used straight from the fridge, so technically “sourdough discard” since it was not active and bubbly. The starter smelled sweet and pleasant indicating a healthy colony of lactic acid bacteria (LABs). In the future, I’d like to experiment with a stiff starter for cookies to observe the flavor and texture difference.

Theoretically, since the flour in the starter is hydrated with water, the enzymes will be more active than in a cookie dough that does not use a water-based liquid (which is most recipes). So, my hypothesis is that with an overnight rest, this recipe will produce a cookie with more complex flavor (from hydrated flour) and deeper color (from the sugars being broken down) than a recipe without starter.

Indeed the sourdough added complexity in the cookie dough, but what I did not expect was the dramatic change in structure. With my classic recipe, the cookies I expect to see out of the oven will be crispy on the outside and gooey on the inside. The initial crunch was the same, but to my surprise, the inside of this cookie had an open crumb that was light and springy, similar to a cake. Overall, I would definitely experiment more with adding sourdough starter to cookies and the initial 100g added may have been too much but now we have better insight on the upper boundary. The recipe now reflects the addition of 50g starter instead, along with adding 25g of flour and 25g of sugar to equalize the dry to wet ingredient ratio.
